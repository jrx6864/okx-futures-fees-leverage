## What a $10,000 position actually costs

Fees are the easy part to calculate. Take a **$10,000 notional** position at 10x, which means $1,000 of your own margin. At the base taker rate of 0.05%, each side costs $5. Open and close at market and you pay **$10 round trip**, or 1% of the margin you posted. Use limit orders and the maker side charges $2 per fill instead.

Funding is where the arithmetic gets less obvious. OKX settles most perpetuals every **8 hours**, and runs some contracts on **1, 2 or 4-hour** intervals. Your cost per settlement is the funding rate multiplied by notional. At 0.01% on a $10,000 position that is $1. An 8-hour contract bills that three times a day; a 1-hour contract bills it 24 times. Because the per-interval rates on short-interval contracts are quoted separately, compare the daily total on the specific contract you are trading rather than the headline percentage. Funding also flips sign, so when the rate is negative, shorts pay longs and you may collect instead of paying.

That gives you a usable rule. A scalp held for minutes is a taker-fee question, and the difference between 0.05% and 0.02% matters more than anything else on this page. A position held across days is a funding question, and you should total the settlements over your expected holding period before you decide which venue is cheap.

## Liquidation, the $3.02B insurance fund, and when ADL fires

OKX publishes the pieces most exchanges leave in a help-centre footnote: actual maintenance margin requirements, the liquidation threshold, the liquidation price formula, the insurance fund balance and the auto-deleveraging process.

The insurance fund is the buffer that absorbs losses when a position is closed at a price worse than its bankruptcy price. OKX’s figure was **US$3,022,801,304** in the disclosure cited on its risk page in mid-October 2026. For scale, the equivalent published figures were about US$1.29 billion for Binance’s USDⓈ-M contracts, roughly US$387 million for Bybit’s USDT-margined fund and about US$177 million at Deribit. A bigger fund means more room before the exchange has to reach for anyone else’s positions.

That reach is ADL. If the loss on a liquidated position exceeds what the fund allocated to that contract can absorb, the exchange reduces opposing positions, typically the most profitable and most heavily leveraged ones, at the bankruptcy price. The practical consequence for you: in a fast one-directional move, a winning trade can be closed early even when your own margin was never at risk. Lower leverage and avoiding contracts where nearly everyone is positioned the same way both reduce the odds of being on the wrong side of that queue.
